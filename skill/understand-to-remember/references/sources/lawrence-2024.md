# Lawrence, Guitard & Cowan (2024): short-term retention of words as a function of encoding depth

- Citation: Lawrence, C. O., Guitard, D., & Cowan, N. (2024). Short-term retention of words as a function of encoding depth. _Memory & Cognition, 52_(6), 1338-1356.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-024-01546-3 (free to read at Springer; author manuscript in PubMed Central, PMC12168558)
- Read: full text, from the PubMed Central author manuscript at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12168558/. Every number below comes from it, and the paper reports its results mostly as test statistics and Bayes factors.

## Design

The study asked whether deeper encoding makes a word last longer in memory over a few seconds, as the levels-of-processing view would predict. Each trial showed four words, one at a time. For each word, the person answered a question about it. The question was about print color (shallow), rhyme (middle) or category membership (deep). A filled delay of arithmetic followed, lasting 0, 3, 6, 12 or 18 seconds. Then the person typed the four words in order. Lists were scored by position, and also as all four words correct.

Four experiments were run. Experiment 1 had 60 online participants (Prolific) and showed each word for 1,500 ms. Experiment 2 had 60 different participants and dropped the orienting questions. It varied presentation time (250, 1,500 or 3,000 ms) to see whether fast presentation produces more forgetting. Experiment 3 had 60 online participants and used 750 ms per word. Experiment 4 repeated Experiment 3 in the lab with 30 university students.

## Findings

- Question type affected recall in Experiments 1, 3 and 4. Category judgments gave the best recall. Rhyme judgments gave the worst, and color judgments fell between them or matched the category condition.
- Experiment 1 showed little loss across the delay. Bayesian evidence favored no interaction between question type and delay (a Bayes factor of 0.006, about 167 to 1 for the null).
- Shorter presentation in Experiment 2 produced more loss over the delay. The effect of delay was reliable on both scoring methods, with an interaction between presentation time and delay on one method only.
- With 750 ms per word (Experiments 3 and 4), recall fell as the delay grew, and question type still mattered. The interaction was again absent, with null evidence between about 27 to 1 and 100 to 1 across the tests.
- In plain terms, deeper encoding raised the starting level of recall but did not slow loss over 0 to 18 seconds. This goes against the original levels-of-processing expectation that deep traces fade more slowly.
- The authors report roughly 10% loss across 18 seconds in Experiments 2 to 4.

## Limits

The delays reached only 18 seconds, and the loss was modest, so a slower decay for deep traces might show up over longer intervals. The authors say the arithmetic task may not have produced the ideal amount of forgetting. The rhyme task may have disrupted the sound-based memory for the list, so the shallow-to-deep ordering did not match older long-term studies. Experiments 1 to 3 were online, and the lab sample in Experiment 4 was smaller. The lists had four words, and recall was explicit, so the results may not carry over to incidental learning.
