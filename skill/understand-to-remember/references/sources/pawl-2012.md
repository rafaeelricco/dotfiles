# Pawl et al. (2012): what MIT seniors remember from freshman physics

- Citation: Pawl, A., Barrantes, A., Pritchard, D. E., & Mitchell, R. (2012). What do seniors remember from freshman physics? _Physical Review Special Topics - Physics Education Research, 8_(2), 020118.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.8.020118 (open access, CC BY 3.0)
- Read: full text, from the APS article PDF at https://journals.aps.org/prper/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.8.020118. Every number below comes from it.

## Design

The authors invited 486 MIT students who had passed freshman mechanics in fall 2005 and were still enrolled in spring 2009. Fifty-six took part. They were paid at least $75 and were not told the subject was physics. Each person had up to 4.5 hours. The materials were a survey, an attitude questionnaire and the Mechanics Baseline Test (MBT, 26 items). They also sat a final exam of 7 multiple-choice and 4 written problems.

Students were sorted by major into three groups by how likely they were to use mechanics later. Group 1 (26 students) had majors least related to physical science, so it is the cleanest test of forgetting without review. Scores were compared with the same students' freshman results, or with other freshmen where no matched data existed.

## Findings

- Group 1 lost 59% (plus or minus 4) of the knowledge they had shown on the written analytic problems at the end of the course. None of them beat their freshman score. Group 2 lost 41% and group 3 lost 3% (plus or minus 13), which was not a reliable change.
- The mean MBT score was 17.6 for seniors and 17.1 as freshmen after the course, so the average showed no change.
- That average hid two patterns. On 9 of the 26 MBT items, mostly graphical kinematics, seniors gained, with a normalized gain of about 70%. On the other 17 items they lost about 50% of what the course had taught.
- On five advanced multiple-choice questions (rotation, oscillation and similar), seniors averaged 32% against 65% for different freshmen, about 51% lower.
- Of 11 students who answered a later effort survey, 10 reported high effort on the multiple-choice items. Five of the 11 reported low or reduced effort on the written problems.

## Limits

Only 56 of 486 invited students took the retest, and all came from one university. The retest used different written problems from the 2005 final, so the authors rescaled scores on the assumption that freshman cohorts are alike. They have no matched freshman scores for the multiple-choice questions, so that result is a comparison with other freshmen, not a measured loss. Effort was checked with a small, voluntary survey, and some students admitted lower effort on the written problems. The study has no group that was taught differently, so it shows how much is lost, not which teaching method prevents it.
