# Cepeda et al. (2006): a meta-analysis of distributed practice in verbal recall

- Citation: Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. _Psychological Bulletin, 132_(3), 354-380.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354 (paywalled at APA; a free copy sits on the first author's site)
- Read: full text, from the first author's own site, https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.pdf. Every number below comes from it.

## Design

The authors searched PsycINFO (1872 to 2002), ERIC (1966 to 2002), reference lists, and earlier reviews. They also asked researchers for unpublished data. They reviewed 427 articles and kept those where adults or children learned verbal material and then took a recall test. Each item had to be studied at least twice. Clinical samples were excluded.

That left 317 experiments in 184 articles, with 839 comparisons of distributed practice and 169 effect sizes. Most comparisons gave only accuracy differences, because the variance data for Cohen's d were missing. The authors compared massed with spaced study and compared different interstudy intervals (ISI). The retention intervals ran from seconds to years. They also compared expanding with fixed intervals.

## Findings

- Across all retention intervals, spaced study gave 47.3% correct and massed study gave 36.7%. This rests on 271 differences from 254 studies and 14,811 participants.
- Only 12 of those 271 comparisons showed no effect or a negative effect of spacing. The authors saw no hint that massing won at any retention interval, short or long.
- For a given retention interval, accuracy first rose and then fell as the ISI grew. The best ISI grew with the retention interval. Under 1 minute, an ISI under 1 minute worked best. At 6 months or more, an ISI of at least 1 month worked best.
- Every study with a retention interval over 1 month favored spreading study over weeks or months rather than 1 day. The average benefit in those studies was 15%.
- Expanding intervals scored 62.0% against 58.6% for fixed intervals. The difference was not reliable (t(42) = 0.5, p = .61). The authors say there is too little data to tell whether expanding schedules help.

## Limits

Eighty percent of the differences used a retention interval under 1 day, and only 4% used one over 1 month. The authors say studies with weeks, months, or years of delay are sorely needed. They could not say how long the ISI should be for a given goal.

The review covers verbal recall only and leaves out recognition tests and skill learning. Different studies fill different bins, so task difficulty could differ between bins. The authors note that the file drawer problem cannot be measured.
