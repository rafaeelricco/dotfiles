# Yang et al. (2021): a meta-analysis of classroom quizzing

- Citation: Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review. _Psychological Bulletin, 147_(4), 399-435.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309 (paywalled, APA; author manuscript free at https://hdl.handle.net/10486/725400)
- Read: full text of the author manuscript, from the Universidad Autonoma de Madrid repository (https://hdl.handle.net/10486/725400). Every number below comes from it. The manuscript is the pre-publication version, so figures in the printed article may differ slightly.

## Design

The authors searched Web of Science, PubMed, PsycARTICLES, PsycINFO, Google Scholar, and a dissertation database. They also checked reference lists and emailed 134 authors for unpublished work. They kept English-language studies in which content was taught in a real class and quizzing was compared with no quizzing or with fewer quiz questions. Laboratory studies and distance education were excluded. The final set was 223 studies, 574 effects, and 48,582 students. They pooled effects as Hedges' g with a multilevel random-effects model. They then tested moderators, including what the control group did, quiz format, feedback, number of quizzes, and school level.

## Findings

- Quizzing raised achievement by g = 0.499 overall. The median effect was 0.448. Of the effects, 82.9% were positive, 15.5% were negative, and 1.6% were zero.
- The size of the gain depended on the comparison. Against no activity or a filler task, g = 0.610. Against restudying, g = 0.329. Against elaborative strategies such as concept mapping, note-taking, and summarizing, g = 0.094, which was not significant.
- Feedback helped. Quizzes with corrective feedback gave g = 0.537, and quizzes without it gave g = 0.374.
- Recognition quizzes (g = 0.520) and recall quizzes (g = 0.516) gave about the same gain.
- Items quizzed once gave g = 0.445, twice 0.599, and three or more times 0.644.
- Quizzing also helped on material that was not quizzed (g = 0.322) and on reworded exam questions (g = 0.559).
- Publication bias checks pointed to little risk. One adjusted estimate was g = 0.457.

## Limits

Effects varied a lot between studies, Q(573) = 4,822. Many moderator groups are small. The authors note that only 6 effects came from continuing education. They also note that some subjects are barely covered, for example 1 effect in physics. Within-subjects designs gave larger effects (g = 0.673) than between-subjects designs (g = 0.415), so design choice affects the estimate. The authors call the elaborative-strategy comparison thin because few effects fed it.
