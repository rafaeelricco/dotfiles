# Morris & Cook (1978): when first-letter mnemonics aid recall

- Citation: Morris, P. E., & Cook, N. (1978). When do first letter mnemonics aid recall? _British Journal of Educational Psychology, 48_(1), 22-28.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1978.tb02366.x (paywalled at Wiley)
- Read: abstract only, from the Crossref record at https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1978.tb02366.x. The Wiley page blocked access. Every number below comes from the abstract.

## Design

First-letter mnemonics are popular, but several studies had found no gain in recall. Experiment I compared mnemonics made by the experimenter with mnemonics made by the subjects, and mnemonic words that were concrete with ones that were abstract. It used 30 undergraduates. Experiment II asked 36 undergraduates to remember a random ordering of the days of the week, with and without a first-letter mnemonic.

## Findings

- Experiment I found no improvement in recall from the mnemonic, whoever made it and whether its words were concrete or abstract.
- In Experiment II, the mnemonic considerably improved recall of the shuffled days of the week.
- The authors suggest that earlier failures may have come from using unrelated words as the material. The mnemonic may help retain order but not help retrieve the items themselves.
- So the mnemonic helps when the items are already known and the order is the hard part.

## Limits

This note covers the abstract only, so details on scoring, materials and recall figures are missing. The samples were small groups of undergraduates. The positive result comes from a single task, the days of the week, where the items are familiar. The abstract does not say whether the effect lasts after a delay.
