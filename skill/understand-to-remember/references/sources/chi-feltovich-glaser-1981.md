# Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981): how experts and novices categorize physics problems

- Citation: Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., & Glaser, R. (1981). Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices. _Cognitive Science, 5_(2), 121-152.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2 (paywalled at Wiley; a scan sits on the Learning and Cognition Lab page at Arizona State University)
- Read: full text, from the lab-hosted scan at https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/classiccitation_1.pdf. The scan has no text layer, so it was read through OCR. Every number below comes from it.

## Design

The paper reports four small studies from the University of Pittsburgh. In Study One, eight physics PhD students (experts) and eight undergraduates (novices) sorted 24 textbook problems. The novices had just finished a semester of mechanics. Both groups sorted by similarity of solution, without pencil and paper. They sorted twice and then explained their groups.

Study Two used 20 new problems that crossed surface features (pulleys, springs, inclined planes) with governing principles (force, energy, momentum). Some pairs looked alike but needed different principles. The paper reports the sorts of a novice, a graduate student, an advanced undergraduate, and a physics professor. In Study Three, two experts and two novices were given category labels and had three minutes to say what they knew about each. In Study Four, two experts and two novices thought aloud about the basic approach to each of the 20 problems.

## Findings

- Experts and novices made about the same number of groups (8.4 and 8.6). The four largest groups held 80% of the problems for experts and 74% for novices.
- Experts sorted by the law that solves the problem, such as conservation of energy or Newton's second law. Novices sorted by objects and literal terms, such as springs or inclined planes. Only 5 of 20 distinct category labels were used by both groups.
- On the first sort, experts took longer than novices, about 18 minutes against 12 minutes.
- In Study Two, the novice's groups followed surface structure. The advanced undergraduate showed a mix of both. The professor grouped by principle.
- In the protocols, the expert's category came with explicit solution methods and the conditions for using them. The novice's category held details of the physical setup but no solution procedure.
- In Study Four, the two experts nearly always named the same principle. The experts cited derived features, such as a before and after situation. Novices cited the literal features in the problem.

## Limits

The authors call these exploratory studies. The samples are very small, and Studies Three and Four used four people each. All problems came from introductory mechanics. The authors say the novices' categories might have been made up on the spot during sorting. They also say their claims about how the representation is built are interpretations.
