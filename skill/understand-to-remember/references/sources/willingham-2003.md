# Willingham (2003): why lessons end up as shallow knowledge

- Citation: Willingham, D. T. (2003, Summer). Students remember... what they think about. _American Educator_.
- Original: https://www.aft.org/ae/summer2003/willingham (free to read, all rights reserved)
- Read: full text, from the article page above. The numbers come from the column's own account of two older experiments; it reports no new data.

## Argument

This is a magazine column answering a teacher's question. A well-built lesson often comes back at test time as isolated facts or as a bare conclusion. Willingham says what a learner remembers is the product of what the learner thought about while meeting the material. His shorthand is "Memory is as thinking does."

He backs this with two experiments. In Hyde and Jenkins, people heard a word list and either rated each word's pleasantness or counted the letter E. They did not know a recall test would follow. The pleasantness raters recalled many more words. They also tended to recall associated pairs, such as doctor and nurse, together. In Barclay and colleagues, a sentence led people to think about one feature of a noun, such as the weight of a piano. People remembered about three times as many nouns when the test hint matched the feature they had thought about.

He lists reasons students end up with shallow knowledge. Some are early in learning, some stop too soon, and some think learning means memorizing exact phrases. Others thought about something other than the lesson's point. His advice for teachers:

- Anticipate what an assignment will make students think about.
- Use discovery learning carefully, because wrong discoveries are remembered as well as right ones.
- Have students process the text actively.
- Build lessons and tests around the lesson's goal.

## Limits

This is an expert column, not a study. The experiments are older work described briefly, with the figures left out here. Willingham says cognitive science cannot yet say exactly how to get students to think about meaning, and he leaves that to experienced teachers. The Hyde and Jenkins task used word lists, so transfer to classroom lessons is argued rather than tested.
