# Dunlosky et al. (2013): a review of ten learning techniques for students

- Citation: Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. _Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14_(1), 4-58.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266 (paywalled at SAGE)
- Read: abstract only, from the PubMed record 26173288 (NCBI E-utilities XML). The full text sits behind a bot check, so it was not read. The note holds only what the abstract states, and the abstract reports no effect sizes.

## Argument

This is a monograph, not an experiment. The authors reviewed the research on ten learning techniques that students can use on their own. The ten are elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, summarization, underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, rereading, practice testing, distributed practice, and interleaved practice. Some were chosen because students use them often.

For each technique, they asked whether its benefits hold across four kinds of variables. Learning conditions covers things like studying alone or in a group. Student characteristics covers age, ability, and prior knowledge. Materials range from simple concepts to complicated science texts. Criterion tasks cover memory, problem solving, and comprehension. They then rated each technique as high, moderate, or low in utility.

## Findings

- Practice testing and distributed practice got high ratings. They help learners of different ages and abilities, and they improve many kinds of outcome, including in school settings.
- Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice got moderate ratings. Their benefits generalize across some variables, but the evidence was limited. The authors name missing tests in educational contexts for the first two, and say interleaving has only begun to be studied.
- Summarization, underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, and rereading got low ratings. The authors say rereading and underlining do not reliably raise performance. They suggest practice testing in place of rereading.

## Limits

Only the abstract was read, so the evidence behind each rating is not summarized here. The ratings are the authors' judgments about how far each technique generalizes. A low rating does not mean the technique never works. The authors say summarization and imagery help some students on some tasks. The moderate ratings reflect gaps in the evidence, not proven failure. The abstract does not say which outcomes were measured, so it cannot show whether a rating applies to deep understanding or to recall.
