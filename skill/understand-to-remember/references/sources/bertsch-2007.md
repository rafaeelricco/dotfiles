# Bertsch et al. (2007): a meta-analysis of the generation effect

- Citation: Bertsch, S., Pesta, B. J., Wiscott, R., & McDaniel, M. A. (2007). The generation effect: A meta-analytic review. _Memory & Cognition, 35_(2), 201-210.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441 (free to read at Springer)
- Read: full text, from the free PDF on the Springer article page that https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441 resolves to (opened in a browser session). Every number below comes from it.

## Design

The authors searched PsycINFO (1966 to 2005), reference lists and unpublished work. They kept studies that compared generated material with material that was only read. Generating meant, for example, completing a word from a rule. The final set was 86 studies, 445 effect sizes and 17,711 participants. Each effect size is the difference between the generate and read proportions remembered, divided by the spread of the read scores. The authors coded 11 moderators. These included test type, intentional or incidental learning, within or between subjects design, stimulus type, retention interval and how much of the target was generated.

## Findings

- The overall effect was d = .40 (95% CI .38 to .42), with generated items remembered at .559 and read items at .471.
- Test type mattered. Effects were .46 for recognition, .55 for cued recall and .32 for free recall.
- Incidental learning gave .65 and intentional learning .32. Within subjects designs gave .50 and between subjects designs .28.
- Numbers as stimuli gave .87, words .41 and nonwords .05. Anagrams gave a small negative effect of -.05.
- Generating the whole target gave .55, and generating part of it gave .32.
- Effects were larger after more than a day (.64) than on an immediate test (.41). Sets of more than 50 items gave only .09.
- The authors see partial support for a mental effort account, but say effort alone cannot explain the pattern.

## Limits

The method corrected only for sampling error, so the authors say their effect sizes underestimate the true effect. Many primary studies lacked reliability data. Several moderator groups were small, such as 18 effects for older adults. The moderators overlap, since easy tasks, number tasks and recall tests tend to travel together. Most studies used word lists and short delays, so the results say little about classroom material. The authors ask for replication as more studies appear.
