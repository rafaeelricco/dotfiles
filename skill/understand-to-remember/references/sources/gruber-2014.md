# Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014): curiosity states and memory, with fMRI

- Citation: Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D., & Ranganath, C. (2014). States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit. _Neuron, 84_(2), 486-496.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060 (free to read; author manuscript in PubMed Central, PMC4252494)
- Read: full text, from the PubMed Central author manuscript (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/). The supplement was not read. Every number below comes from the main text.

## Design

Two experiments used the same procedure. In a screening phase, each person saw trivia questions. They rated how sure they were of the answer and how curious they were about it, on six-point scales. From a pool of 375 questions, each person got 56 high-curiosity and 56 low-curiosity questions they did not already know.

In the study phase, each question appeared, followed by a delay of about 14 seconds before its answer. During the delay a neutral face appeared and the person judged whether that person would know the answer. After the study phase came a surprise recognition test with the 112 studied faces and 56 new ones. A written recall test for the answers followed. The first experiment ran in an MRI scanner with the tests about 20 minutes later. The follow-up behavioral experiment used a delay of 22.5 hours.

The main text gives no sample sizes. The degrees of freedom imply about 18 to 19 people in the scanner experiment and 27 to 28 in the behavioral one.

## Findings

- Recall of answers was higher for high-curiosity questions after 20 minutes (70.6% versus 54.1%) and after one day (45.9% versus 28.1%).
- Faces shown during high-curiosity delays were recognized slightly better (Pr 42.4% versus 38.2%, scanner experiment). After one day the gain held for confidently recognized faces (35.2% versus 31.2%) but not for overall hit rate (39.6% versus 38.0%, not significant).
- Activity in the nucleus accumbens and the left midbrain region (SN/VTA) rose with curiosity ratings while the question was on screen. Hippocampal activity did not.
- Activity during the wait predicted later recall of high-curiosity answers in the nucleus accumbens and the right hippocampus. It did not predict recall of low-curiosity answers.
- People differed a lot in how much curiosity helped their memory for faces. Those differences tracked midbrain and hippocampal activity before the face appeared, and the link between the two regions.

## Limits

The authors call the face effect small and variable between people. Curiosity was self-rated, not assigned, so questions people found interesting may differ from the others in other ways. The brain results are correlations. Trivia questions only loosely stand in for real interests, which the authors say may mean the effects are underestimated in daily life. The tests were recall and recognition of trivia answers and faces, not understanding of course material. The sample was small.
