# Hyde & Jenkins (1973): word recall after meaning-based versus form-based orienting tasks

- Citation: Hyde, T. S., & Jenkins, J. J. (1973). Recall for words as a function of semantic, graphic, and syntactic orienting tasks. _Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 12_(5), 471-480.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(73)80027-1 (paywalled, Elsevier)
- Read: publisher page excerpt, from the ScienceDirect page that the DOI above resolves to. The page blocked direct access, so only the opening text returned by a web search was read. It has no participant counts or scores, so this note has no numbers.

## Design

The opening text describes a line of experiments by Hyde, Jenkins and colleagues on highly associated word lists. People performed an orienting task on each word, and then recalled the words. Semantic tasks needed attention to meaning: pleasant or unpleasant ratings, finding nouns for adjectives, estimating frequency of use, and active or passive ratings. Nonsemantic tasks needed attention to the written or sound form: letter checking, word length, rhyming and number of syllables. Some people knew a recall test was coming and some did not. The excerpt names two tasks added in this study: assigning parts of speech and fitting words into sentence frames. Recall amount and associative structure in recall were the outcomes.

## Findings

- Semantic orienting tasks gave recall as large and as organized as simply being told to learn the list, with no task.
- Tasks that did not require thinking about meaning gave much poorer recall and little associative structure.
- Knowing about the recall test in advance made little difference to recall or structure, and the task effects looked the same either way.
- The authors read this to mean that a meaning-based task makes the semantic links between words available to organize recall.

## Limits

The methods and results sections were not read. This note cannot say how the new tasks fared, how many people took part, or what lists were used. Several of the statements above summarize earlier experiments that the excerpt describes, so check the paper before relying on them as this study's results. The materials are word lists, and recall is tested right after study, so the work does not show effects on understanding or long-term retention.
