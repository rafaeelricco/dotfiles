# Peng, Logie & Della Sala (2024): levels of processing and rates of forgetting

- Citation: Peng, N., Logie, R. H., & Della Sala, S. (2024). Effect of levels-of-processing on rates of forgetting. _Memory & Cognition, 53_(2), 692-709.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-024-01599-4 (open access, CC BY 4.0)
- Read: full text, from the Europe PMC copy of PMC11868305 (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11868305/fullTextXML). Every number below comes from it.

## Design

Three experiments tested whether words processed deeply are forgotten more slowly than words processed shallowly. English-speaking adults judged 40 concrete nouns, one at a time. The deep task asked whether the word fit a sentence. The shallow task asked about rhyme (Experiments 1 and 3) or about its consonant-vowel pattern (Experiment 2). Memory was a yes/no recognition test, scored as d prime.

Each person took an immediate test on half the words. A second test on the other half followed. The delay differed between people. It was 30 min, 2 h or 24 h in Experiments 1 and 2. In Experiment 3 it was 30 min, 2 h or 6 h. Splitting the words this way limited the testing effect. Experiment 1 analysed 86 of 90 recruits and Experiment 2 had 72. Experiment 3 analysed 114 of 120, with depth varied between people instead of within them. Forgetting rates were compared by the depth-by-delay interaction and by fitting retention curves.

## Findings

- Deep processing helped at once. Immediate d prime for deep versus shallow was 2.61 versus 2.10 in Experiment 1 and 2.58 versus 1.83 in Experiment 2. In Experiment 3 it was 2.83 versus 2.11. The mean partial eta squared was 0.43.
- The deep advantage was still there at the delayed tests, with a mean partial eta squared of 0.31. For example, in Experiment 3 at 6 h, d prime was 1.43 for deep and 0.66 for shallow.
- The depth-by-delay interaction was not significant in any experiment (p = .341, .336 and .331 for the delayed tests). The advantage did not grow or shrink with time.
- Slopes of fitted power curves did not differ in Experiments 1 and 2 (p = .134 and .262). In Experiment 3 they did (p < .001), but a logarithmic fit gave no difference (p = .747). The authors blame the power function's built-in link between intercept and slope.
- The authors conclude that forgetting rates do not depend on how deeply an item was encoded. They suggest retrieval practice and spacing are needed to slow forgetting.

## Limits

The materials were unrelated single words, tested by recognition, with delays of 24 h at most. The authors say this limits how far the result reaches into real learning, and they suggest connected material such as stories for later work. The retention curves fitted only moderately, with R squared between about .3 and .6. The slope result in Experiment 3 depended on which curve was used. The two encoding tasks also differed in time taken, which the authors tried to offset in Experiment 2.
