# Bisra et al. (2018): a meta-analysis of self-explanation prompts

- Citation: Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis. _Educational Psychology Review, 30_(3), 703-725.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x (paywalled, Springer)
- Read: abstract only, from the Springer article page that https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x resolves to. Every number below comes from the abstract.

## Design

The authors define self-explanation as learners generating inferences about causal links or conceptual relationships. They searched bibliographic databases for research in which participants received self-explanation prompts while studying or solving problems. Sixty-nine effect sizes from 64 research reports met the inclusion criteria. They pooled them with a random effects model. They also coded and analyzed 20 moderators. These included type of learning task (problem solving, studying worked problems, studying text), subject area, level of education, type of inducement and length of treatment.

## Findings

- The overall weighted mean effect size was g = .55.
- The authors conclude that self-explanation prompts can be a powerful intervention across a range of instructional conditions.
- They note that instructor-scripted prompts limit the approach. They recommend research on computer-generated prompts.

## Limits

Only the abstract was read, so the moderator results, the outcome measures and the delays are not covered here. The abstract does not say how long after study the outcomes were tested. The included studies used prompts written by instructors, so the result may not hold for learners who explain without being prompted.
