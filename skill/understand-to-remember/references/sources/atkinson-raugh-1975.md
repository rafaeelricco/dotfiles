# Atkinson & Raugh (1975): the keyword method for learning Russian vocabulary

- Citation: Atkinson, R. C., & Raugh, M. R. (1975). An application of the mnemonic keyword method to the acquisition of a Russian vocabulary. _Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, 1_(2), 126-133.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.1.2.126 (journal version paywalled, APA; free to read as an author copy on eScholarship)
- Read: full text, from https://escholarship.org/content/qt47j5c59f/qt47j5c59f.pdf, the PDF behind the eScholarship record (fetched through r.jina.ai because the host blocked curl). Every number below comes from it.

## Design

Fifty-two Stanford undergraduates, none of whom had studied Russian, learned 120 Russian nouns by computer. The words were split into three sets of 40, one set per day on Days 1 to 3. Each day had three study and test trials. Half the students were told to use the keyword method. The experimenters supplied an English keyword that sounds like part of the Russian word, and students pictured it interacting with the translation. The other students were told to study however they liked. A test on all 120 words came on Day 4. A repeat came 30 to 60 days later (43 days on average), with no warning.

## Findings

- Before the manipulation, the groups scored alike on the practice phase (55% for the keyword group, 56% for the control group).
- On the Day 4 test, the keyword group got 72% correct and the control group 46% (F(1, 48) = 35.8, p < .001).
- On each day, the keyword group learned at least as many words in two study trials as the control group learned in three.
- On the delayed test, group means were .43 for the keyword group and .28 for the control group.
- Imageability of the English word changed control scores (.55 for the most imageable level, .38 for the least) but not keyword scores (.71 to .75).
- Keyword phrases and single keywords worked about equally well (.74 vs .71 in the keyword group).
- An unpublished follow-up found that learners did a little better with experimenter-supplied keywords than with ones they made up.

## Limits

The control group had no instruction, so the comparison tests the method plus the instruction to use imagery. Women scored higher than men, and the participants were volunteers. The words were concrete nouns for beginners. The authors say the method may help mainly in the early stages of learning a language. It may also suit some learners and word types more than others. The self-generated keyword result comes from an unpublished study.
