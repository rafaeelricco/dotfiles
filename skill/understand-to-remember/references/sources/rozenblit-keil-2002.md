# Rozenblit & Keil (2002): the illusion of explanatory depth

- Citation: Rozenblit, L., & Keil, F. (2002). The misunderstood limits of folk science: An illusion of explanatory depth. _Cognitive Science, 26_(5), 521-562.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1 (free to read as an author manuscript on PubMed Central)
- Read: full text, from the PMC author manuscript at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/. Numbers for Studies 1 to 5 come from the text. Numbers for Studies 6 to 12 come from the paper's own summary table and discussion.

## Design

The paper reports 12 studies of whether people overrate how well they understand how things work. In Studies 1 to 4, students first learned a 7-point scale for depth of understanding. They then rated 48 devices and phenomena (T1). Next they wrote a step-by-step explanation of four or eight of them and re-rated (T2). A diagnostic question that needed real mechanism knowledge followed, with another rating (T3). They then read an expert explanation and rated their earlier understanding (T4) and their new understanding (T5).

Study 1 had 16 Yale graduate students. Study 2 had 33 Yale undergraduates. Study 3 had 16 students at a nonselective regional university, and Study 4 had 32 Yale undergraduates with new devices. In Study 5, 12 other undergraduates rated the explanations from Study 2. Later studies compared devices with facts (capitals), procedures, movie plots and natural phenomena.

## Findings

- Self-ratings fell after people tried to explain. In Study 1 the effect of time was significant, F(4, 56) = 16.195. Study 2 gave the same pattern, F(4, 124) = 38.9. Studies 3 and 4 also showed significant drops.
- After the expert explanation, ratings of new understanding rose sharply (T5). So participants had not simply lost confidence in general.
- In Study 5, independent raters judged the explanations at a mean of 2.72. Study 2 participants had rated themselves 3.89 at T1 and 3.10 at T2, then 2.49 at T3 and 2.62 at T4. The outside view matched the later self-ratings, not the first.
- Per the summary table, procedures and movie plots showed no drop, and facts showed a significantly smaller drop. Natural phenomena dropped like devices. Overconfidence for devices was best predicted by how visible the internal parts were.

## Limits

All samples were students, and several were small, with 16 in Studies 1 and 3. The studies measure confidence in understanding, not how much anyone remembers later. Only Study 5 gives an outside check on the explanations. Study 4 left out the expert explanation and its two ratings to save time. The authors call their account of why the illusion arises, based on how visible the parts are, suggestive and not settled.
