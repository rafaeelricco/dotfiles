# Craik & Tulving (1975): ten experiments on depth of processing and word memory

- Citation: Craik, F. I. M., & Tulving, E. (1975). Depth of processing and the retention of words in episodic memory. _Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 104_(3), 268-294.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.104.3.268 (paywalled at APA; a copy sits on the Rotman Research Institute site)
- Read: full text, from the Rotman Research Institute (Baycrest) publication page copy, https://www.rotman-baycrest.on.ca/files/publicationmodule/@random45f5724eba2f8/JEP75.pdf. Every number below comes from the text of Experiments 1, 2, 5, and the discussion.

## Design

Students saw one word at a time for 200 milliseconds in a tachistoscope. Before each word, they heard or read a yes or no question that forced a certain kind of processing. Structural questions asked about print (is the word in capitals). Phonemic questions asked about rhyme. Semantic questions asked about category or whether the word fit a sentence frame.

The subjects thought the study was about perception and reaction time. After the question phase, they were given an unexpected memory test. The test came after a brief rest. It was recognition in Experiments 1, 2, and 5, and recall in others. Experiment 1 had 20 college students, Experiment 2 had 24, and Experiment 5 had 24. Later experiments tested whether processing time, intention to learn, and reward could explain the results.

## Findings

- Deeper questions took slightly longer to answer, about 150 to 200 milliseconds more for semantic than structural questions in Experiment 1.
- Recognition rose steeply with depth. In Experiment 1 it went from under 20% after structural questions to 96% for sentence questions answered yes.
- In Experiment 2, recognition of yes words rose from 15% after case questions to 81% after sentence questions. For no words it rose from 19% to 49%.
- Time did not explain this. In Experiment 5, a hard structural task took about 1.7 seconds against 0.85 seconds for an easy sentence task. Recognition for yes words was still lower, 57% against 82%.
- Words with yes answers were remembered better than words with no answers at deeper levels. The authors tie this to elaboration. No answers were remembered as well as yes answers when the question was equally congruous for both.
- The pattern held under intentional learning and with a slow presentation rate. The authors conclude that "a minimal semantic analysis is more beneficial than an extensive structural analysis."

## Limits

All items were single common nouns studied one at a time, and the subjects were university students. The memory tests came soon after study, with no test at a long delay. Depth was first indexed by response time. The authors found that this index fails when a task is complex. They say "depth" is a useful description but "elaboration" or "spread" of encoding fits the results better. They also say the framework risks saying only that meaningful events are well remembered.
