# Hake (1998): a survey of 6,542 students in interactive-engagement and traditional physics courses

- Citation: Hake, R. R. (1998). Interactive-engagement versus traditional methods: A six-thousand-student survey of mechanics test data for introductory physics courses. _American Journal of Physics, 66_(1), 64-74.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1119/1.18809 (paywalled, AIP; the author posted his accepted manuscript on his Indiana University page)
- Read: full text of the author's accepted manuscript, from the Internet Archive capture of his Indiana University page (https://web.archive.org/web/2016/http://www.physics.indiana.edu/~sdi/ajpv3i.pdf). Every number below comes from it, and the abstract figures match those of the published version.

## Design

This is a survey of existing course data, not an experiment. Since 1992 Hake had asked physics instructors to send him pre-course and post-course scores. He asked in talks and on email lists. The tests were the Mechanics Diagnostic or the Force Concept Inventory. Both are multiple-choice tests of Newtonian concepts. Some courses also sent a problem-solving posttest, the Mechanics Baseline.

The data cover 62 introductory mechanics courses with 6,542 students in high schools, colleges and universities. Courses that relied mainly on passive lectures, recipe labs and algorithmic exams counted as traditional. Courses that made substantial use of interactive engagement counted as IE. That meant activities in which students think hard (and usually work hands-on) and get immediate feedback from peers or instructors. Instructors' reports and published descriptions decided the label.

The outcome was the average normalized gain, the actual class gain divided by the largest possible gain (100 minus the pretest average).

## Findings

- Fourteen traditional courses (2,084 students) averaged a normalized gain of 0.23 (standard deviation 0.04).
- Forty-eight IE courses (4,458 students) averaged 0.48 (standard deviation 0.14). That is about twice the traditional figure, and the 0.25 difference is 1.8 standard deviations of the IE spread.
- All traditional courses fell in the low-gain band (below 0.3). Of the IE courses, 41 fell in the medium band (0.3 to 0.7), 7 in the low band, and none in the high band.
- IE averages by level were 0.55 for high school, 0.48 for college and 0.45 for university courses. Traditional courses stayed near 0.23.
- Normalized gain barely correlated with pretest score across courses (+0.02), which the author takes as support for using it across different student populations.
- In the 30 courses with the problem-solving test (3,259 students), posttest scores correlated with Force Concept Inventory posttests at r = +0.91. The author infers that IE does not cost problem-solving skill.

## Limits

Instructors chose to send their data. The author says this tends to favor outstanding courses, and unreported low-gain courses are likely missing. Courses were not randomly assigned, and instructors labeled their own methods. The author lists other uncontrolled factors, such as teaching to the test and the Hawthorne effect, and judges it unlikely that they explain the gap. He gives no quantitative estimate of them. The 7 low-gain IE courses show that how well the method is carried out matters. The survey cannot say which IE method works best. The tests cover Newtonian mechanics concepts only, and results are course averages, not individual students.
