# Karpicke & Blunt (2011): retrieval practice versus concept mapping for learning science texts

- Citation: Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. _Science, 331_(6018), 772-775.
- Original: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327 (paywalled at Science; a free author copy is on the Purdue Learning Lab site)
- Read: full text, from the Purdue lab PDF (https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf). It does not include the supporting online material. Every number below comes from the article text.

## Design

Undergraduates learned science texts and were tested on them. In Experiment 1, 80 students studied a text in one of four ways in a single session. The four ways were these. Study once. Study in four consecutive periods. Study, then build a concept map while viewing the text. Study, then practice retrieval by free recall. In the retrieval condition they recalled, restudied, and recalled again. Learning time was exactly matched for the concept mapping and retrieval conditions.

Students then predicted what percentage of the text they would remember a week later. They returned one week later for a short-answer test with verbatim questions (facts stated in the text) and inference questions (which required connecting concepts).

In Experiment 2, 120 students each made a concept map of one text and practiced retrieval on another. Texts had either an enumeration structure (a list of concepts) or a sequence structure (ordered events). Half took the one-week short-answer test. The other half made concept maps from memory, without the text.

## Findings

- In Experiment 1, repeated study, concept mapping and retrieval practice all beat studying once. Retrieval practice did best.
- Retrieval practice (mean 0.67) beat concept mapping (0.45), about a 50% gain (d = 1.50). Concept mapping was not significantly better than extra reading.
- The share of ideas produced at first was nearly the same: 0.78 on concept maps and 0.81 in free recall.
- Students predicted that repeated study would work best and retrieval worst, the reverse of the results.
- In Experiment 2, retrieval practice again beat concept mapping on the short-answer test (d = 1.07) and on the concept-map test (d = 1.01). Verbatim and inference questions gave similar results, so they were pooled.
- Of 120 students, 101 (84%) did better after retrieval practice than after concept mapping. Also, 90 (75%) expected concept mapping to work as well or better.

## Limits

The final test came one week after learning, with undergraduates and short science texts. The condition that used concept mapping asked students to build maps while viewing the text. The authors note that mapping done without the text would amount to retrieval practice. Only one elaborative activity was tested, so the results do not cover other kinds of elaboration. In Experiment 2, students' maps at first held more ideas than their free recall did (0.74 versus 0.65), a gap that favored mapping. The retrieval condition also included a second study period and a second recall.
