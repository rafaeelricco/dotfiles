# Willingham (2008): three memory principles to teach students

- Citation: Willingham, D. T. (2008-2009, Winter). What will improve a student's memory? _American Educator_, 17-25.
- Original: https://www.aft.org/ae/winter2008-2009/willingham (free to read, all rights reserved)
- Read: full text, from the article PDF linked on that page (https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/willingham_0.pdf). The numbers come from the column's summary of one older study of children's study time.

## Argument

This is a magazine column answering a teacher whose students review notes and still do poorly. Willingham says students rarely learn how memory works, so he offers three principles.

- Memories are the residue of thought. You remember what you thought about, and only the part you gave real attention to. Wanting to remember matters little. Teachers usually want meaning retained, so students need a task that forces them to think about meaning. Examples are asking why after a sentence, writing out main ideas, or drawing a hierarchy or a second outline. Copying notes and rereading do not guarantee this.
- Forgetting is mostly caused by missing or ambiguous cues. The column advises distinctive cues, spreading study over time, and studying beyond the point of knowing the material. A rule of thumb is to add about 20 percent more time. Mnemonics help meaningless material by supplying a cue and something to think about.
- People overrate their own knowledge. In one study, fourth and fifth graders gave themselves on average 68 percent of the study time they needed. Willingham advises testing yourself the way you will be tested, for example by explaining the material to another person.

Sidebars cover classroom demonstrations of each principle and four myths of memory, such as sleep learning and the video-recording view of memory.

## Limits

This is an expert column, so its claims rest on studies it cites rather than on new data. The 20 percent figure is labelled a rule of thumb, and the right amount depends on the retention goal and the test. Willingham says the "ask why" strategy is shown for short passages and may not carry over to long chapters. The column does not rank the strategies against one another.
